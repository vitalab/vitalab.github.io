# Highlights

- **AMID** is an autonomous multi-agent framework for medical imaging model development.
- **Data-Conditioned Method Planning (DCMP)** turns task and data evidence into executable method lanes instead of relying on a generic training recipe.
- **Verification-Guided Two-Stage Optimization** combines broad exploration with selective exploitation and prevents unverified evidence from driving model selection.
- On 20 ReX-MLE medical imaging tasks, AMID produces an accepted result for every task, improves over the strongest listed agent baseline on 19 tasks, and ties it on the remaining task.

![AMID's two-stage optimization and overall benchmark performance.](/article/images/AMID/two-stage-optimization-and-performance.jpg)

# Motivation

Large language model (LLM) agents can already plan, write code, run experiments, debug failures, and use empirical feedback to improve a solution. Translating this workflow to medical imaging is difficult because the appropriate preprocessing, model family, validation protocol, metric, and prediction artifact depend on the imaging modality, clinical objective, annotation semantics, and patient-level data organization.

Two limitations are particularly important:

- **The search space is often too coarse-grained.** A task described as classification, segmentation, or detection may require very different methods depending on its modality, anatomy, geometry, and output contract.
- **Experimental feedback is expensive and potentially unreliable.** Medical imaging experiments may require long GPU runs, while data leakage, an incorrect split, a metric mismatch, or a malformed prediction artifact can produce misleading evidence.

AMID turns a medical imaging task and its data into a **high-performing and auditable model package** containing executable code, validation evidence, and submission-ready prediction artifacts.

# AMID framework

![Overview of AMID. The system profiles the task and data, builds executable method lanes, coordinates multiple workers, and verifies candidates before final selection.](/article/images/AMID/amid-system-overview.jpg)

The framework follows five steps:

1. Establish the task and artifact contract.
2. Profile the task and the available data.
3. Construct executable method lanes through Data-Conditioned Method Planning.
4. Coordinate workers through a Self-Organizing Agent Loop and Verification-Guided Two-Stage Optimization.
5. Accept a final model package only when its evidence and artifacts satisfy the required protocol.

## Task and Artifact Contract

The input is intentionally minimal: a medical imaging dataset, a definition of the target output, an evaluation metric, and, when applicable, a submission protocol. The expected output is not a suggested architecture or a single validation score, but a complete model package containing training and inference code, model weights or checkpoints, validation scores, predictions, submission files, and an audit trail.

## Data-Conditioned Method Planning

Selecting a generic training recipe from the task statement alone is rarely sufficient in medical imaging. DCMP makes planning conditional on the actual task, data, and resources that workers can execute.

### Task and data profiling

AMID inspects task descriptions, README files, sample submissions, metric definitions, grader-facing hints, visible training data, labels, metadata, and required prediction artifacts. The resulting profile records:

- **Inventory:** file types, directory layout, train/test organization, identifiers, missing or duplicated cases, and visible strata.
- **Image representation:** dimensionality, channels, data type, intensity range, spacing, orientation, affine consistency, anisotropy, and volume size.
- **Supervision:** class balance, foreground ratio, connected components, object counts and sizes, paired-data alignment, and modality missingness.
- **Evaluation:** metric direction, fold or group constraints, patient-level leakage risks, submission schema, and format checks.

These diagnostics become search predicates. For example, sparse 3D vessel masks suggest patch sampling, spacing normalization, topology-aware post-processing, and memory-aware inference, while histopathology data suggest tiling, stain-variation handling, and slide- or patient-level aggregation.

### Resource-grounded method search

AMID grounds candidate methods in resources that workers can actually use. A pre-seeded local library may include medical imaging toolkits such as **nnU-Net** and **MONAI**, while an external registry provides foundation models and specialized resources such as **MedSAM**, **MedSAM2**, **TotalSegmentator**, and **VISTA3D**. A resource is assigned to a lane only when it matches the profiled modality, task family, data geometry, access conditions, and output contract.

The data profile and resource evidence form a hybrid method graph. Its nodes represent data conditions, method families, and implementation resources; its edges explain why a method family is plausible under the observed constraints.

### Executable method lanes

The output of DCMP is a portfolio of executable method lanes rather than a single best guess. It may include:

- a conservative nnU-Net or MONAI baseline;
- a foundation-model adaptation lane;
- a task-specific architecture lane;
- a preprocessing- or post-processing-heavy lane;
- an ensemble lane;
- calibration-only lanes for validating data loading, metric computation, and submission mechanics.

Each method lane specifies a modeling hypothesis, the data conditions that motivate it, implementation resources, preprocessing assumptions, validation obligations, an approximate training budget, expected artifacts, and likely failure modes. Lower-confidence, mismatched, paper-only, or over-budget ideas are downranked, kept as fallback lanes, or excluded.

## Verification-Guided Two-Stage Optimization

Medical imaging tasks often admit several qualitatively different solution families. AMID therefore delays commitment to a single direction and performs optimization in two stages.

### Stage 1: Behavior-gated Exploration

Workers are distributed across method families and submit attempts tied to specific method lanes. Each attempt records committed code, predictions or submission artifacts, a validation score, metadata, and lane attribution. The goal is to obtain enough comparable behavior from the major lanes to identify feasible and promising candidates.

Exploration is behavior-gated by an independent reviewer. A worker can propose and implement a solution, but it cannot certify its own evidence. Incomplete runs, malformed predictions, duplicate payloads, tune-only checks, or metric-incompatible submissions may inform diagnosis, but they do not count as valid lane coverage.

### Stage 2: Selective Exploitation

The second stage concentrates computation on promoted candidates:

- **Optimizer workers** improve the leading recipe through changes to the model, training, inference, test-time augmentation, post-processing, or ensembling.
- **Challenger workers** preserve a distinct promoted lane when Stage 1 does not identify a clear winner.
- **Repair workers** address promising candidates that fail protocol or artifact checks.
- **Finalizer workers** prepare the final prediction, submission, or model package from an accepted anchor.

### Reviewer gate

The reviewer gate remains active in both stages and checks:

- the validation protocol, split, and fold usage;
- metric direction and computation;
- prediction or submission schema and completeness;
- traceability from committed code to logs, scores, checkpoints, and final artifacts.

An invalid attempt may guide repair or further experimentation, but it cannot drive promotion or final selection. The final model package must be both **high-performing and reviewer-verified**.

## Self-Organizing Agent Loop

Each worker remains autonomous in implementation, debugging, and evaluation, while a central lifecycle manager allocates research effort using evidence stored in shared memory. Workers run in isolated git worktrees but operate against the same task contract, method lanes, attempts, notes, skills, resources, and audit artifacts.

Shared memory is persistent file-system state rather than a private chat transcript. It contains attempt records, scores, commit hashes, experiment notes, method-lane files, resource manifests, validation state, reviewer reports, final-artifact records, and reusable skills. Later workers can therefore reuse successful code paths, avoid known failures, and inspect why a candidate was promoted or rejected.

The lifecycle manager can reinforce productive directions, merge redundant efforts, interrupt or reassign stalled workers, retire saturated branches, and move promising candidates toward finalization. Reflection, consolidation, and plateau heartbeats realign long-running workers without resetting their sessions.

# Experiments

## Experimental setup

AMID is evaluated on **ReX-MLE**, a benchmark containing 20 medical imaging tasks drawn from MICCAI-style and related challenge settings. The tasks cover segmentation, detection, classification, image-quality assessment, and enhancement across panoramic X-ray, CT, MRI, CTA, MRA, histopathology, ultrasound, and microscopy data.

Unless otherwise specified, the main experiments use:

- Codex as the coding-agent runtime;
- GPT-5.5 as the underlying model;
- a 24-hour wall-clock budget per challenge;
- one NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48 GB of memory.

An attempt counts only when its final artifact is accepted under the benchmark contract.

## Main results

![Comparison between AMID, autonomous MLE baselines, and the human reference across the 20 ReX-MLE tasks.](/article/images/AMID/rex-mle-results.jpg)

- AMID produces a valid, accepted result for **all 20 tasks**.
- It improves over the strongest listed autonomous MLE baseline on **19 out of 20 tasks**.
- It ties the strongest baseline on the remaining task, TopCoW-CTA-Cls.
- In a focused comparison where AIDE, ML-Master, and R&D-Agent all use GPT-5.5, the best AMID result remains higher on DENTEX, PUMA-T1-Seg, and TopCoW-MRA-Cls.

The paper also compares AMID with human reference performance from the original competitions. This comparison is approximate because the challenge test sets and the ReX-MLE splits are not identical.

![Accepted AMID performance normalized by the human reference from each original competition.](/article/images/AMID/performance-vs-human-reference.jpg)

AMID reaches or exceeds the approximate human reference on DENTEX, NeurIPS-CellSeg, PUMA-T2-Det, and LDCT-IQA, and approaches it on SEG.A. Larger gaps remain in graph classification, some small-vessel analyses, and ultrasound enhancement.

## Representative cases

- **DENTEX:** AMID combines a compact YOLOv8 detector with anatomical calibration that maps unordered detections to FDI quadrants and tooth numbers. The solution reflects that tooth enumeration is both a visual detection problem and an anatomical ordering problem.
- **PUMA tissue segmentation:** AMID selects pathology foundation encoders and task-specific post-processing rather than training a generic CNN from scratch. Track 1 uses frozen UNI features, while Track 2 fine-tunes the final Phikon-v2 block and combines predictions from multiple crops.
- **PUMA nuclei detection:** the selected pipelines separate proposal generation from classification, using CellViT-256 or PathoSAM proposals with pathology embeddings and lightweight classifiers.
- **NeurIPS-CellSeg:** AMID fine-tunes Cellpose-SAM and obtains an accepted score near 0.90.
- **SEG.A and ISLES'22:** the search returns to strong task-appropriate nnU-Net-style segmentation pipelines with conservative anatomical post-processing.

## Failure modes

- **USenhance:** a stable five-fold pix2pix-style U-Net ensemble is selected over slower and less stable CycleGAN-style candidates. The fixed budget favors strong short-horizon evidence even though the task may benefit from longer adversarial training.
- **TopCoW classification:** the graph-label objective depends on accurate segmentation and topology extraction for very small vessels. Strong voxel-level segmentation is not sufficient when classification depends on small branch contacts.

# Discussion

**Medical model development as a search problem.** AMID searches over executable and verifiable candidates instead of treating model development as a single prompt-to-code task. Data-Conditioned Method Planning makes the candidate space inspectable before expensive experimentation.

**Verification as the control layer.** Runnable code may still use the wrong split, compute the wrong metric, leak patient-level information, or produce malformed artifacts. The reviewer gate changes the optimization process by preventing unverifiable candidates from driving promotion or final selection.

**How AMID differs from prior systems.** AMID targets general medical imaging model construction rather than a single clinical or imaging domain, optimizes competing modeling strategies through broad exploration and selective exploitation, and audits artifacts before model packages are promoted, rejected, or accepted based on executable evidence.

**Scope beyond challenge submissions.** The deliverable could be a trained model package, Dockerized inference pipeline, prediction service, or evidence bundle for human review. However, benchmark performance is system-level evidence, not clinical validation: it does not establish clinical safety, prospective generalization, calibration under deployment shift, or suitability for patient care.

**Limitations.** AMID is expensive in GPU time and coding-agent tokens, and the paper does not yet provide exhaustive method-lane or verification ablations. Its performance also depends on the resource registry, the accuracy of data profiling, and the ability of the underlying coding-agent runtime to implement strong methods under a fixed budget. The authors identify cost-aware early filters, stronger stopping rules, reusable skill distillation, cached resource audits, and selective escalation as important next steps.

# Key takeaways

- AMID treats medical imaging model development as a search over executable method lanes rather than as a single prompt-to-code task.
- Its independent reviewer gate prevents invalid validation results and malformed prediction artifacts from driving model selection.
- AMID outperforms the strongest listed autonomous MLE baseline on 19 of the 20 evaluated ReX-MLE tasks, but its computational cost and incomplete ablations remain important limitations.
